\begin{abstract}
Security Operations Centres (SOCs) face an acute bottleneck in manual threat
enrichment, classification, and response generation. This paper presents a
fully local, privacy-preserving Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) system that
automates the end-to-end intelligence pipeline through a five-agent
architecture augmented by Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG). The system
ingests heterogeneous threat data (CSV, JSON, free text, live social-media
feeds), enriches each event using a locally deployed 3-billion-parameter large
language model (LLM) grounded in a 691-technique MITRE ATT\&CK knowledge base,
clusters events into threat patterns via weighted Jaccard similarity, and
generates D3FEND-mapped mitigation recommendations subject to deterministic
safety validation and human-in-the-loop approval. Evaluation on a 20-event
test corpus demonstrates a TTP extraction rate of 90\%, mean enrichment
confidence of 0.847, classification confidence of 0.912, mitigation relevance
of 0.883, and high-severity event coverage of 92.3\% — all exceeding defined
targets. RAG grounding improved enrichment confidence by 36.4\% over
unaugmented inference and eliminated hallucinated MITRE technique identifiers.
The system requires no cloud infrastructure, no GPU, and no external API keys,
making it deployable on commodity workstations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The global cost of cybercrime is projected to reach \$10.5 trillion annually
by 2025~\cite{cybersecurityventures2023}. Advanced Persistent Threat (APT)
groups employ increasingly sophisticated techniques — living-off-the-land
attacks, supply chain compromises, and AI-assisted intrusion tooling — while
the global cybersecurity workforce faces a shortfall of 4 million
professionals~\cite{isc22023}. SOC analysts are routinely overwhelmed by
thousands of daily alerts, the majority requiring manual contextualisation to
determine relevance, severity, and appropriate response.

Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI) platforms such as MISP, OpenCTI, and
ThreatConnect aggregate threat data from heterogeneous sources but rely heavily
on manual analyst input for enrichment and correlation. This reliance on human
labour creates a latency gap between threat detection and response that
adversaries actively exploit.

Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) open a path to automating
these cognitively demanding tasks. Instruction-tuned LLMs~\cite{ouyang2022instruct}
can extract structured information from unstructured text, map behaviours to
standardised frameworks, and generate contextualised
recommendations~\cite{zhao2023survey}. Motlagh et al.~\cite{motlagh2024llm}
survey the current state of LLM applications across cybersecurity domains,
confirming that threat enrichment and response generation are among the most
tractable tasks. However, ungrounded LLMs hallucinate~\cite{lewis2020rag} —
in the security context, fabricating non-existent MITRE technique identifiers
or CVE numbers can be more dangerous than providing no enrichment at all.
Domain-adapted language models such as CySecBERT~\cite{bayer2022cysecbert}
demonstrate that cybersecurity-specific pre-training improves extraction
quality, motivating our use of MITRE ATT\&CK retrieval as a domain grounding
mechanism.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A five-agent CTI pipeline that automates ingestion, enrichment,
        clustering, mitigation generation, and safety validation end-to-end.
  \item A RAG subsystem that grounds every LLM call in a locally indexed
        MITRE ATT\&CK v18.1 knowledge base (691 techniques), demonstrating a
        36.4\% improvement in enrichment confidence and zero hallucinated
        technique identifiers compared to unaugmented inference.
  \item A self-learning loop in which enriched events are persisted back into
        ChromaDB, enabling the system to reason from historical analogues.
  \item A reproducible 5-metric benchmark framework (EvaluatorAgent) for
        systematic quality tracking across development iterations.
  \item A privacy-preserving deployment model requiring no cloud
        infrastructure, no GPU, and no external API keys.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Automated IOC and TTP extraction.} Liao et al.~\cite{liao2016ioc}
proposed a knowledge-base-driven framework for mining IOCs from threat reports,
demonstrating that structured knowledge priors significantly improve extraction
precision. Husari et al.~\cite{husari2017ttpdrill} introduced TTPDrill, a
rule-based NLP pipeline for extracting threat actions from CTI reports mapped
to ATT\&CK tactics. Their approach achieves high precision on well-structured
reports but requires manually curated extraction rules. Our approach replaces
rule engineering with LLM inference grounded by semantic retrieval,
generalising to free-form and social-media text.

\textbf{LLM-based security analysis.} Brown et al.~\cite{brown2020gpt3} showed
that large autoregressive language models exhibit strong few-shot reasoning over
domain-specific text with no task-specific fine-tuning. Zhao et
al.~\cite{zhao2023survey} survey LLM capabilities including structured
information extraction and reasoning over domain knowledge. Touvron et
al.~\cite{touvron2023llama2} present Llama 2, the open-weight model family
underpinning the llama3.2:3b variant used in this work. Fang et
al.~\cite{fang2024llm} demonstrate that LLM agents can autonomously identify
and exploit one-day vulnerabilities, confirming their applicability to
offensive security — and by extension, to defensive enrichment tasks. Unlike
these works, our system operates entirely locally to preserve data
confidentiality.

\textbf{Retrieval-Augmented Generation.} Lewis et al.~\cite{lewis2020rag}
introduced RAG as a mechanism for grounding LLM outputs in non-parametric
knowledge, showing significant reductions in factual hallucination on
knowledge-intensive NLP tasks. Gao et al.~\cite{gao2023rag} survey RAG
architectures and identify semantic retrieval quality as the primary
determinant of downstream generation quality. Our work applies these findings
to the CTI domain, using cosine-similarity-filtered MITRE ATT\&CK retrieval
as the grounding mechanism.

\textbf{LLM agent reasoning.} Yao et al.~\cite{yao2023react} introduced
ReAct, a paradigm that interleaves chain-of-thought reasoning with action
execution in LLM agents, enabling more grounded and verifiable behaviour.
Our Mitigation Agent adopts this pattern explicitly: the prompt sequences
threat-mechanism reasoning, D3FEND countermeasure selection, and
minimally-scoped action generation before producing a structured output.

\textbf{Multi-agent CTI systems.} Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023agents} survey
LLM-based autonomous agent architectures, identifying separation of concerns
between specialist agents as a key design principle. Wooldridge and
Jennings~\cite{wooldridge1995agents} define the theoretical foundations of
intelligent agent systems. Salek et al.~\cite{salek2025cti} present an
automated CTI pipeline on IEEE Access and identify the absence of systematic
evaluation frameworks as a critical gap in context-aware CTI research — a gap
directly addressed by the EvaluatorAgent benchmark in this work. Cheng et
al.~\cite{cheng2025ctiarena} propose CTIArena, a benchmark for evaluating
LLM-based CTI systems, further motivating standardised quality measurement.
Our five-agent pipeline operationalises these principles with each agent
owning a single pipeline stage and communicating via typed Pydantic schemas.

\textbf{Gap addressed.} Prior work either (a) requires cloud LLM APIs,
violating SOC data-handling policies; (b) addresses isolated sub-tasks rather
than the full enrichment-to-response pipeline; or (c) lacks a systematic
evaluation framework. This work addresses all three gaps simultaneously.

\subsection{Data Models}

All inter-agent communication uses typed Pydantic v2 schemas forming the
pipeline chain:
\texttt{ThreatEvent} $\to$ \texttt{EnrichedEvent} $\to$
\texttt{ThreatPattern} $\to$ \texttt{MitigationAction}.

\noindent\textbf{ThreatEvent} captures: \texttt{event\_id} (MD5 hash),
\texttt{event\_type} (IP / URL / Domain / Hash / CVE / Email),
\texttt{severity} 1--5, \texttt{raw\_text}, \texttt{indicator},
\texttt{source}, and \texttt{timestamp}.

\noindent\textbf{EnrichedEvent} extends \texttt{ThreatEvent} with
LLM-extracted fields: \texttt{ttps[]} (MITRE technique IDs),
\texttt{iocs[]}, \texttt{cves[]}, \texttt{malware\_family},
\texttt{attack\_vector}, \texttt{attack\_stage}, and
\texttt{enrichment\_confidence} $\in [0.0,\,1.0]$.

\subsection{Asynchronous Orchestration}

The \texttt{CTISystem} orchestrator runs a 5-second polling loop using Python
\texttt{asyncio}. The FastAPI/Uvicorn web server executes concurrently in the
same event loop. All LLM calls are wrapped in \texttt{asyncio.to\_thread()},
preventing the 45--90 second inference window from blocking dashboard responses.

\section{Agent Design and Key Algorithms}
\label{sec:agents}

\subsection{Agent 1 — Ingestion}

The ingestion agent normalises four input formats (CSV via pandas, JSON arrays
and objects, URL-fetched text, and free-form text) into \texttt{ThreatEvent}
objects. Deduplication uses MD5 hashing of \texttt{source} $+$ \texttt{indicator}
content, preventing reprocessing of identical indicators.

Threat type detection evaluates indicators in priority order: IPv4 regex
($\texttt{(\textbackslash d\{1,3\}.\{3\}\textbackslash d\{1,3\})}$),
hex-string length $\in \{32, 40, 64\}$ for hashes, URL prefix, CVE prefix,
email pattern, domain heuristic, and \texttt{UNKNOWN} fallback. Severity is
inferred from keyword presence in \texttt{raw\_text} (e.g.,
\textit{``ransomware''}, \textit{``critical''} $\to$ \texttt{CRITICAL}).

\subsection{Agent 2 — Enrichment (LLM + RAG)}
\label{subsec:enrichment}

Enrichment follows a four-step pipeline per event:

\textbf{Step 1 — MITRE retrieval.} The event's \texttt{raw\_text} is embedded
using \textit{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}~\cite{reimers2019sbert}, a sentence
transformer~\cite{devlin2019bert} fine-tuned for semantic similarity
(384-dimensional vectors). ChromaDB~\cite{chroma2024} executes an HNSW approximate
nearest-neighbour search over 691 indexed MITRE ATT\&CK Enterprise v18.1
techniques, returning the top-5 results. Only results with cosine similarity
$\geq 0.35$ are retained; the conversion from ChromaDB L2 distance $d$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{sim}(A,B) = 1 - d(A,B) \label{eq:cosine}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 2 — Past-threat retrieval.} The same query is issued against the
\texttt{threat\_reports} ChromaDB collection (enriched past events), returning
up to 3 similar historical analogues.

\textbf{Step 3 — LLM extraction.} A four-section structured prompt
(system role, MITRE context block, past-threats block, event details and JSON
schema) is submitted to \textit{llama3.2:3b} via Ollama~\cite{ollama2024} at
temperature 0.1. The model extracts: \texttt{iocs[]}, \texttt{ttps[]},
\texttt{cves[]}, \texttt{malware\_family}, \texttt{attack\_vector},
\texttt{affected\_assets[]}, \texttt{attack\_stage}, \texttt{threat\_category},
and \texttt{enrichment\_confidence}.

A three-stage JSON extraction pipeline handles output variability: (i) direct
\texttt{json.loads()}, (ii) regex extraction between \texttt{```json...```}
fences, (iii) raw object scan for first \verb|{| to last \verb|}|.

\textbf{Step 4 — Self-learning store-back.} Events with
\texttt{enrichment\_confidence} $> 0.3$ are persisted into the
\texttt{threat\_reports} ChromaDB collection, enabling future enrichments to
retrieve them as context.

\subsection{Agent 3 — Pattern Discovery}

Events are grouped into \texttt{ThreatPattern} objects using a weighted
multi-factor similarity score:
\begin{equation}
  S(A,B) = 0.4 \cdot J_{\text{TTP}} + 0.3 \cdot \mathbb{1}_{\text{malware}}
           + 0.2 \cdot \mathbb{1}_{\text{vector}} + 0.1 \cdot J_{\text{asset}}
  \label{eq:similarity}
\end{equation}
where $J_{\text{TTP}} = |A_\text{ttps} \cap B_\text{ttps}| \,/\,
|A_\text{ttps} \cup B_\text{ttps}|$ is the Jaccard coefficient~\cite{jaccard1912}
over TTP sets, $\mathbb{1}_{\text{malware}}$ is 1 iff \texttt{malware\_family}
matches, $\mathbb{1}_{\text{vector}}$ is 1 iff \texttt{attack\_vector} matches,
and $J_{\text{asset}}$ is the Jaccard coefficient over \texttt{affected\_assets}.
Event pairs with $S(A,B) \geq 0.7$ are assigned to the same pattern.

Pattern confidence is bounded as:
$\text{confidence} = \min(0.5 + n_{\text{events}} \times 0.1,\ 0.95)$

Jaccard was selected over k-means or DBSCAN because TTPs are discrete
categorical labels (not continuous features), the metric is directly
interpretable by analysts, and it requires no hyperparameter tuning or model
training~\cite{jaccard1912}.

\subsection{Agent 4 — Mitigation Generation}

Mitigations are triggered when \texttt{severity} $\geq 3$ or when a new
\texttt{ThreatPattern} is discovered. The agent uses a chain-of-thought
prompting strategy~\cite{wei2022cot} that explicitly sequences three reasoning
steps:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{Threat mechanism analysis} — entry point, lateral movement
        paths, and adversary objectives.
  \item \textit{D3FEND countermeasure selection} — mapping to MITRE
        D3FEND~\cite{d3fend2023} categories (D3-NI, D3-ITF, D3-OTF, D3-UA,
        D3-MFA, D3-EDR, D3-PA, D3-PM).
  \item \textit{Minimal effective mitigation} — the smallest set of
        proportionate, specific actions addressing the identified mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Agent 5 — Validation}

Validation applies deterministic safety rules without LLM calls, checking:
(i) completeness (title, description, $\geq$2 steps each $\geq$10 characters);
(ii) dangerous commands (\texttt{rm -rf}, \texttt{format c:}, \texttt{drop
database}); (iii) overly broad network rules (\textit{``block all''},
\textit{``deny all ports''}); (iv) disproportionate actions
(\textit{``shutdown all systems''}); (v) unqualified firewall wildcards
(\texttt{0.0.0.0/0} without port context).

\subsection{Key Implementation Challenges}

\textbf{LLM blocking in async context.}
Ollama's Python SDK is synchronous. Wrapping calls in
\texttt{asyncio.to\_thread()} dispatches inference to the default
\texttt{ThreadPoolExecutor}, keeping the event loop free for concurrent
dashboard responses during 45--90 second inference windows.

\textbf{ChromaDB initialisation latency.}
Embedding 691 MITRE techniques requires 45--90 seconds on first run. A
\texttt{collection.count()} guard in \texttt{is\_ready()} skips re-indexing
on all subsequent starts by loading from the persisted
\texttt{data/chroma\_db/} directory.

\textbf{Mastodon HTML content.}
Mastodon API responses contain HTML with anchor tags, line breaks, and
escaped characters that break IOC regex extraction. Python's built-in
\texttt{html.parser} strips tags to plain text; a defang-reversal function
normalises researcher-obfuscated IOCs (\textit{hxxps://},
\textit{[dot]}) before indicator extraction.

\textbf{Feed interference with manual submissions.}
Concurrent Mastodon polling could inject unrelated events during a user's
instant submission. An \texttt{asyncio.Event} gate pauses \texttt{FeedStreamer},
wakes the processing loop early via \texttt{/api/ingest/instant}, and resumes
feeds after the synchronous pipeline completes.

\subsection{Dashboard}

The FastAPI server serves 8 static HTML pages on port 8888 using
vanilla HTML/CSS/JavaScript (no front-end build tooling): Pipeline Overview,
Ingestion, Enrichment, Patterns, Mitigations, Flow Tracker, Live Input, and
Live Feeds. Auto-refresh polls the API every 3 seconds. The Flow Tracker page
traces any event through all five pipeline stages for end-to-end auditability.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

The system was evaluated on a 20-event test corpus spanning four threat
categories: phishing with credential harvesting (T1566, T1078), ransomware
delivery via PowerShell (T1059.001, T1486), APT lateral movement (T1021,
T1550, T1055), and CVE exploitation (T1190, T1059, T1068). Hardware: Intel
Core i7, 16 GB RAM, no GPU. All events were processed through the complete
five-agent pipeline; the EvaluatorAgent computed five objective metrics after
pipeline completion.

\subsection{Performance Benchmarks}

Dashboard API responses averaged 18 ms (max 47 ms) against a 500 ms target.
ChromaDB semantic search over 691 techniques averaged 42 ms (max 89 ms)
against a 200 ms target. During a live 45-second LLM inference call, 10
concurrent dashboard requests all completed within 50 ms, confirming that
\texttt{asyncio.to\_thread()} prevents event-loop blocking. The primary
throughput bottleneck is CPU-only LLM inference at 45--90 s per event
($\approx$40--80 events/hour), an inherent constraint of running a 3B-parameter
model without GPU acceleration.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Design Trade-offs}

Selecting Jaccard similarity over learned clustering algorithms (k-means,
DBSCAN) was a deliberate trade-off: TTPs are discrete categorical labels,
not continuous features, making centroid-based distance metrics ill-suited.
Jaccard is directly interpretable — analysts can inspect precisely which
techniques overlap — and requires no training data, hyperparameter tuning,
or retraining as the threat landscape evolves.

The cosine similarity threshold of 0.35 for RAG retrieval was empirically
validated against live ChromaDB data. Representative results:
\textit{``PowerShell malware execution''} retrieves T1059.001 at 0.619 and
T1546.013 at 0.558; \textit{``phishing email credential theft''} retrieves
T1586.002 at 0.578, T1566 at 0.477, and T1078 at 0.441. Results below
threshold are excluded to prevent noisy context from degrading LLM output.

\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{CPU throughput.} 45--90 s per event limits the system to
$\approx$40--80 events/hour, insufficient for high-volume production SOC queues.
GPU offloading via Ollama's CUDA backend would reduce per-event time to 2--5 s.

\textbf{In-memory state.} Events, patterns, and mitigations are held in RAM
and lost on restart. A SQLite or PostgreSQL persistence layer is required
for production deployment.

\textbf{Language coverage.} Both llama3.2:3b and all-MiniLM-L6-v2 are
English-optimised. CTI in Russian, Chinese, or Arabic — significant
threat-actor sources — may receive lower enrichment quality.

\textbf{Feed breadth.} Live intelligence is limited to Mastodon public
timelines. Production platforms integrate ISAC feeds, VirusTotal, Shodan,
and dark web monitoring.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper presented a fully local, five-agent CTI pipeline that automates
the end-to-end threat enrichment, classification, pattern discovery,
mitigation recommendation, and safety validation workflow. RAG grounding on a
691-technique MITRE ATT\&CK knowledge base eliminates LLM hallucination of
technique identifiers — the primary failure mode of unaugmented LLM-based
enrichment — and delivers a 36.4\% improvement in enrichment confidence.
All five quantitative benchmark metrics exceeded their targets on a 20-event
evaluation corpus. The system operates entirely on commodity hardware without
GPU, cloud services, or external API keys, making it immediately deployable
in privacy-constrained SOC environments.

Future work will focus on: (i) GPU acceleration via Ollama CUDA offloading to
reach sub-5 s per-event throughput; (ii) SQLite/PostgreSQL persistence for
cross-session intelligence accumulation; (iii) TAXII 2.1 client integration
for ISAC feed compatibility; (iv) APT attribution using MITRE ATT\&CK
intrusion-set objects; and (v) multilingual embedding replacement for
non-English CTI coverage.
